  • SEC01

    CIA and the property actually harmed

    [Exam Focus]
    • CIA: property actually harmed
      • Confidentiality: restricted disclosure
      • Credit-card copying violates secrecy
      • Intact original ≠ preserved confidentiality
      • Integrity: unauthorized/improper change prevention
      • Altered balance; inserted false message
      • Availability: timely usable authorized service
      • Crashes; resource starvation; denied access
      • One event may harm multiple goals
      👁 Full explanation
      Confidentiality restricts disclosure: copying a credit-card database violates secrecy even if the original stays intact. Integrity restricts unauthorized or improper changes: altering a balance or inserting a false message damages correctness. Availability supplies timely, usable service to authorized users: crashes, resource starvation and denied access violate it. One event can harm several goals.
    • Identity, evidence and information assurance
      • Authentication: identity or claim establishment
      • Authorization: permitted actions
      • Accountability: responsible entities linked to actions
      • Reliable identity and protected logs
      • Non-repudiation: origin/receipt evidence
      • Signatures ≠ physically impossible denial
      • Assurance: confidence, risk management, protection
      • Detection; response; restoration; information lifecycle
      • Data at rest, in use, in transit
      • Accidental corruption and intentional attacks
      👁 Full explanation
      Authentication establishes an identity or other claim; authorization decides permitted actions. Accountability links actions to responsible entities through reliable identity and protected logs. Non-repudiation supplies evidence supporting origin or receipt; signatures do not make denial physically impossible. Information assurance includes confidence, risk management, protection, detection, response and restoration across the information lifecycle. Data security protects information at rest, in use and in transit, including accidental corruption as well as intentional attacks.

  • SEC02

    Threat, vulnerability, risk and control

    [Exam Focus]
    • Asset, threat, vulnerability, attack, control
      • Assets: data, software, hardware, service, reputation
      • Threat: potentially harmful circumstance
      • Vulnerability: exploitable weakness
      • Attack: actual attempt
      • Control: reduced likelihood or impact
      • Software flaw ≠ active attack
      • Risk: likelihood and consequence
      • Unmeasured labels ≠ universally meaningful product
      👁 Full explanation
      An asset is something valuable: data, software, hardware, service or reputation. A threat is a potentially harmful circumstance; a vulnerability is an exploitable weakness; an attack is an actual attempt; a control reduces likelihood or impact. A software flaw is not automatically an active attack. Risk concerns likelihood and consequence, not a universally meaningful numeric product of two unmeasured labels.
    • Control purposes and categories
      • Preventive: block; detective: reveal
      • Corrective/recovery: restore safe operation
      • Deterrent: discourage attempts
      • Encryption: disclosure prevention
      • Audit log: detection and accountability evidence
      • Physical; technical; administrative categories
      • Categories ≠ competing stages
      • Firewall cannot replace policy or locks
      👁 Full explanation
      Preventive controls block events, detective controls reveal them, corrective/recovery controls restore safe operation, and deterrents discourage attempts. Encryption is preventive for disclosure; an audit log is principally detective/accountability evidence. Physical, technical and administrative are control categories, not competing stages. An external firewall cannot replace a written policy or a locked equipment room.

  • SEC03

    Attack classifications and attacker intent

    • Attack effects and 4D
      • Interception: observe/copy
      • Modification: alter
      • Interruption: block
      • Fabrication: false insertion
      • Disclosure; deception; disruption; usurpation
      • Usurpation: unauthorized control
      • DoS: disruption and availability loss
      • DoS need not obtain control
      👁 Full explanation
      Interception copies or observes data, modification alters it, interruption blocks it, and fabrication inserts false material. The 4D classification uses disclosure, deception, disruption and usurpation, the last meaning unauthorized control. DoS primarily demonstrates disruption/availability loss; it need not give control of the victim.
    • Passive versus active attacks
      • Passive: unchanged target exchange
      • Sniffing; traffic analysis
      • Active: inject, alter, replay, disrupt
      • Passive/active ≠ offline/online
      • Encrypted traffic still exposes timing/size
      • Traffic analysis infers relationships
      • Neither universally easy to prevent/detect
      👁 Full explanation
      Passive attacks observe without changing the target exchange: sniffing and traffic analysis. Active attacks inject, alter, replay or disrupt. These are not synonyms for offline and online. Traffic analysis can infer relationships from timing/size even when content is encrypted. Neither category is universally easy to prevent or detect.
    • Attacker intent and trust position
      • White hat: authorization and scope
      • Black hat: malicious or unauthorized
      • Competitor: commercial secrets
      • Hacktivist: cause
      • Hacker label ≠ legality
      • Insider/outsider: origin and trust position
      • Origin ≠ attack mechanism
      👁 Full explanation
      White-hat testing requires authorization and scope; black-hat activity is malicious or unauthorized. A competitor may seek commercial secrets; hacktivists pursue a cause. “Hacker” alone does not establish legality. Insider versus outsider describes origin/trust position, not the specific mechanism.

  • SEC04

    Malware: propagation, disguise and payload

    • Malware replication and disguise
      • Virus: attaches to program/document host
      • Infected-host execution spreads virus
      • Worm: independent replication, commonly networked
      • Trojan: useful disguise, malicious behavior
      • Trojan need not self-replicate
      • Labels describe different properties
      • Package may contain multiple components
      👁 Full explanation
      A virus replicates by attaching to a host program or document; execution of the infected host spreads it. A worm replicates independently, commonly across networks. A Trojan disguises malicious behavior as something useful and does not require self-replication. These labels describe different properties, so a malware package may contain several components.
    • Malware payloads and defenses
      • Spyware: monitoring; keylogger: keystrokes
      • Adware: unwanted ads, possible tracking
      • Ransomware: encryption/locking and extortion
      • Logic bomb: trigger-dependent
      • Backdoor: ordinary-access bypass
      • Rootkit: hidden presence/privileged activity
      • Zombie/bot: remote commands; botnet: collection
      • Malware: umbrella, not replication method
      • Update; restrict execution; isolate compromise
      • Tested offline backups
      • Antivirus never guarantees complete protection
      👁 Full explanation
      Spyware monitors users; a keylogger records keystrokes. Adware displays unwanted advertising and may also track users. Ransomware denies access through encryption/locking and extortion. A logic bomb waits for a trigger. A backdoor bypasses ordinary access paths; a rootkit hides malicious presence or privileged activity. A zombie/bot follows remote commands; a botnet is a collection. Malware is the umbrella term, not a specific replication method. Update, restrict execution, isolate compromise and maintain tested offline backups; no antivirus guarantees complete protection.

  • SEC05

    Social engineering, spoofing and sessions

    • Social engineering
      • Phishing: trusted-source impersonation
      • Secret theft or induced unsafe action
      • Spear phishing: specific person
      • Verify through independently known channel
      • Shoulder surfing: observed entry
      • Pretexting: invented disclosure reason
      • Authorized monitoring ≠ necessarily attack
      👁 Full explanation
      Phishing impersonates a trusted source to obtain secrets or induce an unsafe action; spear phishing targets a specific person. Verify a request through an independently known channel. Shoulder surfing observes entry; pretexting invents a reason for disclosure. Monitoring software used with authorization is not necessarily an attack merely because it records activity.
    • Spoofing, replay and session attacks
      • Masquerade: another identity
      • ARP poisoning: local IPv4-to-MAC associations
      • DNS spoofing/cache poisoning: false resolutions
      • IP spoofing: false source address
      • Intermediary: interception and possible alteration
      • Replay: reuse valid old message
      • Replay defenses: nonces, timestamps, sequence checks
      • Session hijacking: stolen/predicted valid token
      • Protect transport; regenerate IDs after login
      • Restricted cookies; per-request server authorization
      👁 Full explanation
      Masquerade claims another identity. ARP spoofing poisons local IPv4-to-MAC associations; DNS spoofing/cache poisoning supplies false name resolutions; IP spoofing falsifies a source address. An intermediary attack intercepts and can alter exchanges. A replay reuses a valid old message; nonces, timestamps and sequence checks can reject it. Session hijacking steals or predicts a valid session token. Protect transport, regenerate session IDs after login, restrict cookies and check authorization server-side on every sensitive request.

  • SEC06

    DoS, wireless attacks and layer-specific defenses

    • Resource-exhaustion attacks
      • DoS: exhaust or disable resource
      • DDoS: multiple sources
      • Bots; reflected/amplified responses
      • SYN flood: incomplete-handshake state
      • DNS flooding: query capacity
      • NXDOMAIN: nonexistent-name resolver stress
      • Fragment floods: reassembly/processing exhaustion
      • Rate limits; SYN cookies; filtering
      • Upstream capacity controls
      • Defense depends on bottleneck
      👁 Full explanation
      DoS exhausts or disables a resource; distributed DoS uses multiple sources, often bots or reflected/amplified responses. A SYN flood leaves many incomplete TCP handshakes occupying state. DNS flooding targets query capacity; NXDOMAIN attacks generate queries for nonexistent names to stress resolvers. Fragment floods abuse reassembly or packet-processing resources. Rate limits, SYN cookies, filtering and upstream capacity controls address different bottlenecks.
    • Wireless attacks and defense layers
      • Rogue AP: imitate/bypass approved service
      • Bluesnarfing: Bluetooth information theft
      • Bluejacking: unsolicited Bluetooth messages
      • Phishing not wireless-specific
      • Authenticated IPsec: IP impersonation resistance
      • DNSSEC: DNS-data authentication
      • TLS: application-transport authentication/protection
      • Bounds-safe code: overflow prevention
      • HTTPS cannot repair unsafe memory copy
      👁 Full explanation
      Rogue access points imitate or bypass approved wireless service. Bluesnarfing steals Bluetooth information; bluejacking sends unsolicited Bluetooth messages. Phishing is not specific to wireless. Match a defense to its layer: authenticated IPsec can resist IP impersonation, DNSSEC authenticates DNS data, TLS authenticates/protects application transport, and bounds-safe code prevents overflows. HTTPS cannot fix an unsafe memory copy in a server.

  • SEC07

    Identification, authentication and factors

    [Exam Focus]
    • Access-control sequence and direction
      • Identify → authenticate → authorize → record
      • Identification: username/claim, not proof
      • Identification omitted: authentication first available stage
      • User-to-host: user proves identity to host
      • Host-to-user: host proves identity to user
      • Arrow: prover → verifier
      👁 Full explanation
      A common access sequence is identify the subject, authenticate the claim, authorize the operation, then record relevant events. Identification is the username/claim, not its proof. If identification is omitted from choices, authentication is the first available access-control stage. User-to-host means a user proves identity to a host; host-to-user means the host proves its identity to a user; the arrow names the prover and verifier.
    • Authentication factors
      • Knowledge: password/PIN
      • Possession: token/card
      • Inherence: biometric, fingerprint
      • Behavioral: typing rhythm, handwriting
      • Textbook category: something one does
      • One biometric ≠ three factors
      • Password plus PIN: one factor class
      • Card plus PIN: two factors
      • OTP factor depends on generation/delivery
      👁 Full explanation
      Knowledge is a password/PIN, possession is a token/card, and inherence is a biometric such as a fingerprint. Behavioral biometrics include typing rhythm or handwriting; some textbooks call these “something one does.” A body characteristic is not “three-factor authentication”: one biometric is one factor. Password plus PIN remains one factor class; card plus PIN is two. An OTP’s factor depends on its delivery/generation mechanism.

  • SEC08

    Passwords and delegated authorization

    • Password attacks and protection
      • Brute force: enumerate guesses
      • Dictionary: prioritize likely words
      • Credential stuffing: reuse leaked credentials
      • Online: server rate limits
      • Offline stolen verifiers: no server limits
      • Short secrets vulnerable in either setting
      • Unique long secrets; password manager
      • Rate limiting; suitable MFA
      • Salted slow hashes, not reversible plaintext
      • Public salt defeats shared precomputed lookup
      👁 Full explanation
      Brute force enumerates guesses; dictionary attacks prioritize likely words; credential stuffing tries previously leaked credentials on another service. Online attempts face server rate limits; offline attacks against stolen verifiers do not. Short passwords can fail in either setting. Use unique long secrets and password managers, rate limiting and suitable MFA; store salted slow password hashes, not reversible plaintext passwords. A salt prevents shared precomputed lookup, but is not secret.
    • OAuth authorization framework
      • Client: limited access tokens
      • Authorization server: authentication/owner approval
      • Resource server: protected-data token validation
      • Authorization server may delegate identity checks
      • OAuth alone ≠ complete login identity protocol
      • OpenID Connect: added identity layer
      👁 Full explanation
      OAuth is an authorization framework: a client obtains limited access tokens, the authorization server authenticates/obtains approval from the resource owner, and the resource server validates tokens for protected data. The authorization server can delegate identity checks. OAuth alone does not define a complete end-user login identity protocol; OpenID Connect adds an identity layer.

  • SEC09

    Access control and least privilege

    [Exam Focus]
    • Subjects, objects and ACLs
      • Subjects request operations on objects
      • Object ACL: allowed subjects/actions
      • Network ACL: address, protocol, port matching
      • Direct effects: classification and filtering
      • ACL ≠ guaranteed availability/malware detection
      👁 Full explanation
      Subjects request operations on objects. An ACL associates an object/resource with allowed subjects or actions; a network ACL matches traffic such as address, protocol and port. Classification and filtering are direct effects; high availability or malware detection is not guaranteed by an ACL.
    • Access-control models
      • DAC: owner grants permissions
      • Mandatory: enforced labels and policy
      • Ordinary owners cannot override mandatory policy
      • RBAC: permissions→roles; users→roles
      • ABAC: subject/object/action/environment attributes
      • Rule-based: explicit conditions
      • Time or source-network rules
      👁 Full explanation
      DAC lets owners grant permissions; mandatory control enforces labels and policy that ordinary owners cannot override. RBAC gives permissions to roles and assigns users to roles. ABAC evaluates subject, object, action and environment attributes. Rule-based controls apply explicit conditions, such as time or source network.
    • Privilege restrictions
      • Least privilege: needed rights, needed time
      • Separation of duties: split sensitive steps
      • Deny by default
      • Mediate every access
      • Revoke stale permissions
      • SELECT allowed; UPDATE denied
      • Access control supports integrity
      • Business-state validity needs additional enforcement
      👁 Full explanation
      Least privilege gives only needed rights, for needed time. Separation of duties splits sensitive steps across parties. Deny by default, mediate every access and revoke stale permissions. Allowing SELECT but denying UPDATE is an access-control decision that helps integrity; it does not by itself enforce all valid business-state transitions.

  • SEC10

    Confidentiality and integrity models

    [Exam Focus]
    • Bell–LaPadula confidentiality
      • Simple security: no read up
      • Star: no write down
      • Strong star: same-level writes only
      • TS>S>C>U
      • S subject may read C
      • S subject cannot write C
      • Compartments and DAC may further deny
      👁 Full explanation
      Bell–LaPadula protects confidentiality. The simple-security rule prohibits reading above a subject’s level; the star rule prohibits writing down. The strong-star variant permits writing only at the same level. With TS>S>C>U, an S subject can read C under the simple rule but cannot write C under star; compartments and discretionary restrictions may still deny access.
    • Integrity models
      • Strict Biba: no read down
      • Strict Biba: no write up
      • Prevent low-integrity input contaminating higher results
      • Clark–Wilson: certified well-formed transformations
      • Integrity checks; separation of duties
      • Clark–Wilson ≠ confidentiality model
      • MAC: enforcement category
      • Mandatory policy need not be Bell–LaPadula
      👁 Full explanation
      Strict Biba protects integrity by preventing read down and write up: untrusted lower-integrity input should not contaminate higher-integrity results. Clark–Wilson protects integrity through certified well-formed transformations, integrity checks and separation of duties. It is not a confidentiality model. “MAC” is an enforcement category and does not force every system to use Bell–LaPadula rather than another label policy.

  • SEC11

    Physical protection and resilient design

    • Physical protection and threat models
      • Theft; unauthorized connections
      • Heat; humidity; water; surges; fire
      • UPS: temporary conditioned power
      • Faulty installation/battery: possible hazard
      • Disconnected machine: fewer network paths
      • Insiders; removable media; supply-chain attacks
      • Physical access remains a threat
      • Most-secure claim requires threat model
      👁 Full explanation
      Protect equipment against theft, unauthorized connections, heat, humidity, water, electrical surges and fire. A UPS supplies temporary conditioned power; it is normally protective, but faulty installation/battery failure can itself be hazardous. A disconnected machine loses many network attack paths yet remains exposed to insiders, removable media, supply-chain attacks and physical access. There is no universal “most secure computer” without a threat model.
    • Resilient security design
      • Defense in depth: independent layers
      • Minimization: remove unnecessary software/services
      • Compartmentalization: limit blast radius
      • Fail securely: errors never silently grant
      • Uniformity: easier patching and prediction
      • Uniformity: correlated failures
      • Diversity: reduced common-mode compromise
      • Diversity: higher administration cost
      • Protection proportional to assets and threats
      👁 Full explanation
      Defense in depth layers independent controls. Minimization removes unnecessary software and services; compartmentalization limits blast radius. Fail securely means errors do not silently grant permission. Uniform configurations simplify patching and prediction but create correlated failures; diversity may reduce common-mode compromise while raising administration cost. Choose protection proportional to asset value and credible threats.

  • SEC12

    Cryptographic vocabulary and security assumptions

    • Cryptographic vocabulary
      • Plaintext: original; ciphertext: encrypted representation
      • Key: algorithm input
      • C=E_k(P); P=D_k(C)
      • Symmetric five: plaintext, algorithm, secret key
      • Ciphertext and decryption algorithm
      • Cryptanalysis: unauthorized recovery or breaks
      • Cryptology: cryptography plus cryptanalysis
      • Steganography: hidden existence
      • Encryption: hidden meaning
      👁 Full explanation
      Plaintext is the original message, ciphertext its encrypted representation, a key is input to an algorithm, encryption computes C=E_k(P), and decryption recovers P=D_k(C). The five conventional symmetric ingredients are plaintext, encryption algorithm, secret key, ciphertext and decryption algorithm. Cryptanalysis seeks recovery or other breaks without the authorized secret; cryptology includes cryptography and cryptanalysis. Steganography hides that a message exists; encryption hides its meaning.
    • Cryptographic security assumptions
      • Known algorithm; protected keys
      • Implementation secrecy not main argument
      • b-bit key → 2^b possibilities
      • Uniform exhaustive-search average: half keyspace
      • Algorithm; key size; randomness
      • Mode and implementation matter
      • Symmetric ≠ inherently less secure
      • Speed ratios not fixed constants
      👁 Full explanation
      Assume the algorithm is known and protect keys: secrecy of implementation is not the main security argument. A b-bit key has 2^b possibilities; exhaustive search averages roughly half the space if keys are uniformly random. Security depends on algorithm, key size, randomness, mode and implementation. Symmetric cryptography is not inherently less secure than public-key cryptography, and speed ratios are not fixed constants.

  • SEC13

    Substitution, shift and affine ciphers

    • Substitution versus transposition
      • Substitution: replace symbols
      • Transposition: rearrange positions
      • Monoalphabetic: one mapping throughout
      • Frequency leakage despite large nominal keyspace
      • Classical ciphers: teaching, not modern protection
      👁 Full explanation
      Substitution replaces symbols; transposition changes their positions. A monoalphabetic substitution preserves one mapping throughout the message and leaks frequency patterns despite a large nominal key space. Classical ciphers are teaching devices, not modern data protection.
    • Caesar, multiplicative and affine arithmetic
      • Alphabet mapping: A=0…Z=25
      • Caesar: C=(P+k) mod26
      • Inverse: P=(C−k) mod26
      • k=3: A→D; HELLO→KHOOR
      • k=25: ATTACK→ZSSZBJ
      • Wrap negatives into 0–25
      • Multiplicative: C=kP mod26; gcd(k,26)=1
      • Decrypt with modular inverse of k
      • Affine: C=aP+b mod26; gcd(a,26)=1
      • Inverse relation, not repeated numeric multiplication
      👁 Full explanation
      With A=0 through Z=25, Caesar encryption is C=(P+k) mod26 and decryption P=(C-k) mod26. A shift of 3 maps A→D and HELLO→KHOOR. Key25 moves back one: ATTACK→ZSSZBJ. Ensure negative results wrap into 0–25. A multiplicative cipher uses C=kP mod26 with gcd(k,26)=1; decryption uses k’s modular inverse. Affine uses C=aP+b mod26 and requires gcd(a,26)=1. The inverse-key relation, not simply the same numeric multiplication, recovers plaintext.

  • SEC14

    Vigenère, Playfair and Hill

    • Vigenère and autokey
      • C_i=(P_i+K_i) mod26
      • Repeated or otherwise supplied shifts
      • Same letter may encrypt differently
      • Repeated short keys leak periodicity
      • Autokey extends initial key with message
      👁 Full explanation
      Vigenère repeats or otherwise supplies shifts K_i and computes C_i=(P_i+K_i) mod26. The same plaintext letter may encrypt differently by position; repeated short keys still leak periodic structure. An autokey scheme extends its initial key using message material.
    • Playfair method
      • Keyed 5×5 grid; commonly merged I/J
      • Encrypt letter pairs
      • Specify duplicate fillers and padding
      • Same row: right
      • Same column: down
      • Rectangle: other column, original row
      • Decrypt by reversed shifts
      👁 Full explanation
      Playfair uses a keyed 5×5 grid, commonly combining I/J, and encrypts letter pairs. Specify duplicate-letter fillers and padding. Same row moves right, same column moves down, and rectangle pairs take the other column in their original row; decryption reverses shifts.
    • Hill cipher and known plaintext
      • Letter vectors: C=KP mod26
      • Invertible K modulo26
      • gcd(det(K),26)=1
      • Linear structure
      • Enough independent known blocks reveal key
      • K=CP^-1 mod26
      • Claimed known-plaintext resistance: false
      • Polygraphic substitution ≠ modern strength
      👁 Full explanation
      Hill uses C=KP mod26 for letter vectors. K needs an inverse modulo26, equivalently determinant coprime to26. It is linear: enough independent known plaintext/ciphertext blocks reveal K=CP^-1 mod26. The supplied claim of resistance to known-plaintext attacks is false; polygraphic substitution alone is not modern strength.

  • SEC15

    Transposition and one-time pads

    • Transposition procedures and conventions
      • Rail fence: specified zigzag, read rails
      • Columnar: write rows, ordered columns
      • Symbols and counts preserved
      • Specify rows; column order; padding; direction
      • Inconsistent source-example conventions
      • Two rails ABCDEF: ACE and BDF
      • Ciphertext ACEBDF
      👁 Full explanation
      Rail-fence writes along a specified zigzag and reads rails; columnar transposition writes rows and reads columns in a specified order. They preserve the symbols and their counts. Since source examples use inconsistent conventions, always state rows, column order, padding and direction before computing. Two-rail zigzag for ABCDEF writes ACE on the first rail and BDF on the second, producing ACEBDF.
    • One-time pad conditions
      • XOR with independent uniformly random secret
      • Key at least message length
      • Never reuse key
      • Perfect secrecy requires every condition
      • Reuse: C1 XOR C2=P1 XOR P2
      • Vernam XOR ≠ automatically OTP
      • Predictable/reused keystream loses guarantee
      • Key delivery and storage costs
      👁 Full explanation
      A one-time pad XORs bits with an independent uniformly random secret key at least as long as the message and never reused. Its perfect secrecy depends on every condition. Reusing a pad leaks C1 XOR C2=P1 XOR P2. A Vernam XOR construction is not automatically a one-time pad: a predictable/reused keystream loses that guarantee. Key delivery and storage remain substantial costs.

  • SEC16

    Symmetric keys, AES and distribution

    • Symmetric keys and distribution
      • Shared secret; efficient bulk protection
      • n users: n(n−1)/2 pairwise keys
      • Trusted KDC changes architecture
      • Session key: temporary communication context
      • Master/key-encryption key: protects subordinate keys
      • Physical distribution; authenticated protected channel
      • Trusted third-party distribution
      • Uncompromised old key protects new key
      • Stolen old key prevents secrecy restoration
      👁 Full explanation
      Symmetric encryption uses a shared secret and efficiently protects bulk data. Pairwise independent keys for n users require n(n-1)/2 shared keys; a trusted key distribution center changes that architecture. A session key is temporary for a communication context; a master/key-encryption key protects subordinate keys. Distribute keys physically, via an authenticated protected channel, or through a trusted third party. An uncompromised old key can protect a new key, although this does not restore secrecy after the old key is stolen.
    • AES and cipher construction
      • AES block: 128 bits
      • Keys: 128/192/256 bits
      • Rounds: 10/12/14
      • Substitution/permutation design
      • SubBytes; ShiftRows; MixColumns; AddRoundKey
      • Final round omits MixColumns
      • Block cipher: mode and nonce/IV handling
      • Authenticated encryption detects tampering
      • Stream cipher: data plus keystream
      • Nonce uniqueness when construction requires
      👁 Full explanation
      AES is a 128-bit block cipher with 128/192/256-bit keys and 10/12/14 rounds.     DES and Feistel mechanics

    • DES Feistel mechanics
      • Block: 64 bits
      • Key: 56 effective bits, eight parity
      • 16 rounds; initial permutation
      • L_i=R_(i−1)
      • R_i=L_(i−1) XOR f(R_(i−1),K_i)
      • Right-half expansion: 48 bits
      • XOR with 48-bit subkey
      • Eight S-boxes: six→four bits each
      • Permutation: 32 output bits
      • S-box row: outer input bits
      • S-box column: inner four bits
      👁 Full explanation
      DES has a 64-bit block, 56 effective key bits (eight other bits serve parity), and 16 Feistel rounds. After initial permutation, each round computes L_i=R_(i-1), R_i=L_(i-1) XOR f(R_(i-1),K_i). Its right-half expansion makes 48 bits, XOR mixes a 48-bit subkey, eight S-boxes each map six bits to four, and permutation rearranges the 32 output bits. The outer S-box input bits select a row and the inner four select a column.
    • Feistel inversion and DES limitations
      • Nonlinear S-boxes resist simple linear descriptions
      • Same structure, reversed round keys
      • Round function need not be invertible
      • Final swap/permutation
      • DES and triple DES: legacy
      • Not choices for new protection
      • Three 56-bit keys ≠ 168-bit effective security
      👁 Full explanation
      The nonlinear S-boxes resist simple linear descriptions. Feistel inversion uses the same overall structure with reversed round keys; f need not itself be invertible. Final swapping/permutation completes DES. DES and triple DES are legacy algorithms, not choices for new protection. Triple DES repeats DES operations but does not provide 168-bit effective security merely because three 56-bit keys are supplied.

    Hashes, MACs and signatures

    • Hash versus keyed MAC
      • Arbitrary input → fixed-length digest
      • Deterministic; preimage and collision resistance
      • Collisions necessarily exist
      • Not mathematically unique fingerprints
      • Unkeyed hash can be attacker-recomputed
      • Hash alone needs trusted reference
      • MAC: integrity, shared-secret authentication
      • Both holders can create MAC
      • Limited third-party non-repudiation

      A cryptographic hash maps arbitrary-length input to a fixed-length digest. Restrict small groups and overlapping queries, track query history, coarsen/suppress results, or introduce carefully designed noise; each trades utility against leakage.
    • Multilevel database security
      • Different item labels; mandatory policy
      • Subject clearances; classifications; compartments
      • Bell–LaPadula-style read/write restrictions
      • Prevent direct downward confidentiality flow
      • Trusted declassification: separately controlled action
      • Views alone ≠ multilevel isolation proof
      • Covert channels; query results; error messages
      • Assess direct access and indirect inference
      👁 Full explanation
      A multilevel database stores items at different labels and enforces mandatory policy using subject clearances, classifications and compartments. Bell–LaPadula-style read/write restrictions prevent direct downward confidentiality flow; trusted declassification is a separate controlled action. Views alone are not proof of multilevel isolation. Covert channels, query results and error messages may still reveal information, so evaluate both direct access and indirect inference.

    Policy, incident response and recovery objectives

    • Policy versus procedure
      • Policy: responsibilities and rules
      • Procedure: operational steps
      • Coordinate protection; reduce risk
      • Acceptable use; access review
      • Incident handling; recovery
      • Policy ≠ merely passive system use
      • Awareness: reporting and independent verification
      • Enforcement and usable procedures
      👁 Full explanation
      A security policy states responsibilities and rules; a procedure supplies the steps. Policies coordinate protection, reduce risk and define acceptable use, access review, incident handling and recovery. They do not prescribe merely “passive system use.” Awareness training teaches reporting and independent verification; enforcement and usable procedures make it operational.
    • Incident response and recovery objectives
      • Prepare roles/tools
      • Detect/analyze → contain → eradicate
      • Restore verified service; learn from evidence
      • Preserve useful protected logs
      • Disaster recovery: restore IT
      • Business continuity: sustain business functions
      • Workarounds and alternate facilities
      • RTO: recovery-time target
      • RPO: tolerable lost-data age
      • Backup schedule meets recovery point
      • Restore tests demonstrate recoverability
      👁 Full explanation
      Prepare response roles/tools, detect and analyze, contain the incident, eradicate the cause, restore verified service and learn from the evidence. Preserve useful protected logs. Disaster recovery restores IT after disruption; business continuity sustains business functions through workarounds and alternate facilities. RTO is a recovery-time target; RPO measures the tolerable age of lost data. A backup schedule must satisfy the required recovery point, and a restore test must demonstrate that data can actually be recovered.

    Interpret flawed security questions precisely

    • Flawed security-question interpretation
      • Overlapping/missing mechanisms: no invented unique answer
      • Asymmetric question: multiple wrong key directions
      • Signature ≠ generic private-key whole-message encryption
      • Body biometric ≠ three factors
      • SSL/SSH both describe protected channels
      • Remote-shell scope distinguishes SSH
      • Malformed option labels unrecoverable from theory
      👁 Full explanation
      Do not select an invented unique answer when choices overlap or lack the mechanism. The asymmetric-message question has several wrong key directions; signatures do not generically encrypt whole messages with private keys. Body biometrics do not imply three factors. SSL and SSH can both describe protected channels unless the stem specifies remote shell. A malformed option label cannot be recovered from theory.
    • Missing distinctions in security questions
      • Arbitrary code execution: multiple mechanisms
      • Execution consequence ≠ uniquely buffer overflow
      • Database requirements: availability and confidentiality
      • ACL/RBAC both restrict unauthorized writes
      • Neither alone enforces all integrity constraints
      • Evaluation organizations depend on national scheme
      • CCRA: recognition, not evaluator
      • Supported answer requires missing distinction
      👁 Full explanation
      Undefined “arbitrary code execution” does not uniquely identify buffer overflow: several mechanisms can produce that consequence. A database-security requirement can include both availability and confidentiality. Generic ACLs and RBAC both restrict unauthorized writes but neither independently enforces all integrity constraints. Evaluation organizations must be identified by the actual national scheme; CCRA is recognition, not an evaluator. An answer is supported only after the missing distinction is supplied.
